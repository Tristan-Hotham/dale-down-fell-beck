Dale, down, fell and beck

The words Britain uses for its hills, valleys and streams, mapped. I sorted {{n_classified}} named features by their landscape word to find where beck gives way to brook, how sharp those lines are, and whether they follow the Vikings.

Dr Tristan Hotham, Independent researcher · October 2026 · Data: Ordnance Survey OS Open Names and OS Open Rivers

Walk north through England and the water changes its name. In Surrey a stream is a brook. In Yorkshire it is a beck. In Northumberland it becomes a burn, and in the Highlands an allt. The hills change too: downs in Wessex, tors on Dartmoor, fells in the Lake District, laws in the Borders, bens and cnocs further north.

These words are a fossil map of who named the land. I wanted to know where the lines between them fall, how sharp those lines are, and whether they match history: the Danelaw, the Norse settlement names that end in -by and -thwaite, and the borders with Wales and Scotland.

I took every named valley, hill and waterfall in Ordnance Survey's OS Open Names, and every named watercourse in OS Open Rivers, and sorted each one by its landscape word using explicit matching rules that are published with the data. Of {{n_landscape}} features, {{n_classified}} ({{pct_classified}}) carry a word I could recognise. I added {{n_settle}} towns, villages and hamlets, checked for Norse elements such as -by, -thorpe and -thwaite.

    02 · WaterBrook, beck, burn

    Beck comes from Old Norse bekkr. Brook and burn come from Old English brōc and burna.

    The streams draw the clearest lines in Britain. OS Open Rivers names {{n_streams}} separate watercourses (I joined {{n_links}} river links into single streams). {{n_burn}} are burns, {{n_allt}} are allts, {{n_brook}} are brooks and {{n_beck}} are becks.

    The chart walks up England and southern Scotland in {{band_km}} km steps, leaving Wales aside. Brook rules the south. Then, in a band no wider than a county, beck takes over. A little further north, burn takes over from beck.

    Walking north: each word's share, every {{band_km}} km
    Share among beck, brook and burn in each {{band_km}} km band of northing. England and Scotland only. Bands with fewer than 8 of these words are left blank.

    The beck/brook line runs across northern England, through Lancashire, the south Pennines and Nottinghamshire to Lincolnshire, at a median latitude of {{bb_lat}}°N. It is remarkably clean. {{bb_a_on}} of becks lie on the beck side of the line and {{bb_b_on}} of brooks on the brook side. Across it, the share of becks falls from 80% to 20% in about {{bb_w}} km.

    The burn/beck line is just as sharp ({{bub_w}} km) but sits in the wrong place for a national border. {{bub_in_eng}} of its length is in England, through County Durham and northern Cumbria, a median {{bub_border}} km south of the Scottish border. Northumberland's streams are burns, like Scotland's.

    Northumberland's streams are burns, Yorkshire's are becks, Derbyshire's are brooks. The changes take about a day's walk.

    Further north there is one more line, between Scots burn and Gaelic allt. It runs for {{ab_km}} km, in {{ab_pieces}} pieces, through Argyll and Bute, Perth and Kinross and Highland, and it is softer: the 80% to 20% change takes {{ab_w}} km on average, and anywhere from {{ab_min}} to {{ab_max}} km on different stretches.

    03 · SharpnessHow sharp are the lines?

    For each pair of neighbouring words I fitted a smooth surface of where each is more common, drew the line where they are equally common, and then measured how far you have to travel across it for one word to go from 80% of the pair to 20%. A short distance means a sharp line. The method is in the note at the end.

    Distance over which one word gives way to the other
    Kilometres from 80% to 20%, with 95% bootstrap intervals. Shorter is sharper. Blue = streams, slate = valleys, orange = hills. "Own side" = share of each word on its own side of the line.

    The sharpest lines are the stream lines: beck/brook ({{bb_w}} km) and burn/beck ({{bub_w}} km). The valley words dale and clough meet across Yorkshire and the Peak in {{dc_w}} km. Welsh and English words meet at the Welsh border: nant and afon against brook in {{nb_w}} km, cwm against dingle in {{cd_w}} km.

    The softest are the pairs where one word is rare. Combe and bottom blur over {{cb_w}} km across the West Country, and only {{cb_a_on}} of combes sit on the combe side. Tor and down, both Old English, mix over {{td_w}} km on the edge of Dartmoor.

    04 · Valleys and hillsDale, down, hill and fell

    Dale comes from Old English dæl, reinforced by Old Norse dalr. Combe is a Brittonic word, the same word as Welsh cwm. Fell comes from Old Norse fjall, down from Old English dūn and tor from Old English torr.

    Use the buttons above the walking-north chart to switch to valleys or hills. The valleys sort into neat bands. Bottoms fill Hampshire, Dorset and Wiltshire, combes Somerset and Devon, cloughs Lancashire and the south Pennines, dales Yorkshire, cleughs the Borders and Dumfries and Galloway. Clough and cleugh are the same word in English and Scots spelling, and the dales sit between them. They never meet closely enough for me to draw a line.

    Hills are different. Hill itself is everywhere: {{n_hill}} of the {{n_hillfam}} named hills in the family are just hills. The local words are islands in that sea. Down ({{n_down}}) is southern England, led by Wiltshire, Devon and Cornwall. Fell ({{n_fell}}) is Cumbria, the Pennines and Galloway. Tor ({{n_tor}}) is Devon and Cornwall, with a few in the Peak. Law ({{n_law}}) is the Scottish Borders and Northumberland. Fell gives way to law across the Scottish border over {{fl_w}} km.

    05 · The VikingsDo the lines follow the Norse?

    -by, -thorpe, -thwaite and -toft come from Old Norse words for a farm or settlement, an outlying farm, a clearing and a plot. Some -thorpe names may instead be Old English throp.

    The obvious test for beck is Norse settlement. In every hexagon I took the share of streams that are becks and the share of towns, villages and hamlets with a Norse element in their name. Across {{bvn_n}} English hexagons with enough of both, the two go together strongly: a rank correlation of {{bvn_rho}} (95% interval {{bvn_lo}} to {{bvn_hi}}, from a bootstrap that resamples 100 km blocks, because neighbouring hexagons are not independent).

    The link is strong but not one to one. Where no settlement has a Norse name, {{band0_beck}} of beck-or-brook streams are still becks. Where at least a fifth of settlements do, {{band4_beck}} are. On the beck side of the beck/brook line, {{nbs_beck}} of settlements have a Norse element. On the brook side, {{nbs_brook}}.

    Other words line up too. Across English hexagons, dale's share of valleys correlates with Norse names at {{c_dale}}, beck at {{c_beck}}, gill at {{c_gill}} and fell at {{c_fell}}. Brook ({{c_brook}}) and down ({{c_down}}) go the other way.

    And the Danelaw?

    The treaty between Alfred and Guthrum set a boundary up the Thames, up the Lea to its source, straight to Bedford, then up the Ouse to Watling Street. I drew an approximate version of that line and continued it along Watling Street and then straight to Chester. It is for orientation only.

    The beck/brook line does not follow it. The whole of the beck/brook line lies north of it, a median {{dl_bb_med}} km away and never closer than {{dl_bb_min}} km. In the Danelaw south of 53.2°N, brooks outnumber becks by {{dn_brook}} to {{dn_beck}}.

    The settlement names are a different story. North of 53.2°N almost everything is north-east of the line, so I tested only the part of England the line actually crosses, south of that latitude. There, {{s_base}} of all OS named features lie north-east of the line. So do {{s_norse}} of settlements with a Norse element, {{s_by}} of -by names and {{s_thorpe}} of -thorpe names, against {{s_ton}} of -ton names and {{s_brook}} of brooks.

    Even here the change is a gradient rather than a wall. In the 20 km strip just south-west of the line, {{pf_sw}} of settlements have a Norse element; in the strip just north-east, {{pf_ne}}; and {{pf_far}} at 60 to 80 km. Becks only appear in numbers more than 60 km into the Danelaw, in Lincolnshire and Norfolk.

    The Danelaw line separates the place-names. The stream names change much further north.

    06 · BordersWales stops at the border, Scotland does not

    I derived the borders of England with Wales and Scotland from the country recorded for every OS name. The Welsh words stop almost exactly there. The line between nant or afon and brook runs a median {{nb_border}} km from the Welsh border, and {{nb_border20}} of it lies within 20 km. The cwm/dingle line is a median {{cd_border}} km from the border. A second, separate nant/brook line crosses south Pembrokeshire, where the English-speaking south of the county meets the Welsh-speaking north.

    The Scottish border is not a word boundary at all. The burn/beck line runs a median {{bub_border}} km inside England, and the fell/law line a median {{fl_border}} km from the border, wandering either side of it. The words of the Borders belong to both countries.

    08 · Odd ones outRare words and Norse corners

    Some words are tiny local dialects. Every rife in the data is in West Sussex. Lincolnshire has {{eau_top}} of the {{eau_n}} eaus, a fenland name for a watercourse. Lodes are in the fens of Cambridgeshire and Lincolnshire. Foss is a North Yorkshire waterfall word; elsewhere in the north a waterfall is a force, from Old Norse fors, while Scotland has its linns and Wales its pistylls and rhaeadrs.

    Hidden in the town names

    Stream and valley words also hide inside settlement names: Ashbourne, Blackburn, Holbeck, Ilfracombe. They follow the same order from north to south: burn, then dale and beck, then brook, bourne and combe.

    09 · The whole vocabularyCounting the words

    How many of each word

    One caution runs through all of this. OS Open Names is a modern map, not a historical record. Many names are recent: drains cut in the fens, farm-named hills, cloughs named after the mill above them. A name being on the map tells us what people call a place now, or called it when the surveyors came, not when it was first named. The patterns are still striking, because local words for landscape are exactly the kind of thing new names inherit from old ones.

    How I did this

    Features. OS Open Names, filtered to {{n_osnames}} named features. Valleys are the local types Valley and Cirque Or Hollow ({{n_valleyfam}}). Hills are Hill Or Mountain, Hill Or Mountain Ranges, Cliff Or Slope, Other Landform and Spot Height, plus Other Landcover features named fell or down, because OS records most named downs and fells as open land rather than hills ({{n_hillfam}}). Waterfalls are the type Waterfall ({{n_fallfam}}). Settlements are all populated places ({{n_settle}}).

    Streams. OS Open Rivers splits each river into links between junctions. Counting links would weight long, fragmented rivers more than short ones, so I joined same-named links that share a node or come within 2 km into one watercourse and placed it at the vertex nearest its length-weighted centre. That gives {{n_streams}} watercourses from {{n_links}} links.

    Words. Each name gets at most one landscape word: its generic. In English the generic usually comes last (Mill Beck); in Welsh and Gaelic it comes first (Afon Conwy, Allt Beag, Cwm Idwal); in "Burn of Tafts" it comes before "of". Compounds count too (Borrowdale, Troutbeck, Scafell). The full rules are regular expressions in code/words.py, with tests. Where the main name has no match I tried the Welsh or Gaelic alternative name ({{n_via2}} matches).

    Checking. I read {{audit_n}} random matches, 30 from each family, and judged {{audit_false}} to be wrong. A second check of {{strat_n}} matches drawn only from the rarer words found {{strat_false}} wrong and {{strat_unc}} uncertain. Earlier checks caught Easter and Wester being read as Norse -ster names, and Dunkirk as kirk; the rules now exclude them.

    Shares. {{hex_n}} hexagons {{hex_w}} km across on the British National Grid. A word's share is its count divided by all named features of its family in the hexagon, including those with no recognised word.

    Boundaries. For each pair of words I counted both on a 2 km grid and smoothed each with a Gaussian kernel, choosing the kernel width (6 to 20 km) by leave-one-out likelihood. The line is where the two smoothed surfaces are equal, drawn only on land and where at least 5 kernel-weighted features of either word are nearby. For sharpness, I took every feature of the two words within 60 km of the line, measured its signed distance to the line, and fitted a logistic regression of word on distance. The 80% to 20% distance is 2 ln 4 divided by the slope. Intervals come from 300 bootstrap resamples. The local thickness on the map repeats the fit on 60 km stretches of line.

    Danelaw and borders. The Danelaw line is hand-placed from the treaty description and is approximate. The national borders come from the nearest OS name's country field on a 2 km grid. Correlations are Spearman rank correlations between hexagons with at least 5 settlements and 5 features of the family, with 95% intervals from a 100 km block bootstrap.
